Research

Background

This game grew out of a simple observation from fieldwork inside Dutch public-sector inspection organisations: "Responsible AI" is usually treated as a checklist — registers, documentation, explainability tools — but what actually happens when people sit down and have to use those tools together is much messier, and much more interesting.

The research this game supports asks: how do public-sector teams actually practise transparency while building an AI system — not what the policy says they should do, but what they do when a formal requirement (like a national algorithm register) meets real uncertainty about who's going to ask questions later?

That question is hard to study through interviews or observation alone, because the exact same situation never happens twice across different teams. A game can manufacture that same situation — the same ambiguous event, the same disclosure deadline — and put it in front of different groups, so what they do can actually be compared side by side.

Beyond the Algorithm is Paper 3 of a PhD dissertation on Responsible AI governance in Dutch public-sector inspection, built directly on top of two earlier studies: a review of what conditions make Responsible AI interventions succeed or fail, and an ethnographic study of how those conditions show up in a real inspection organisation. The game takes what that fieldwork surfaced and turns it into something teams can be dropped into and observed navigating, live.

Why a game

Ethnography can tell you what one team, in one place, actually did. It can't easily show you what a different team would have done facing the exact same dilemma — because that dilemma never repeats itself in the wild.

A game can. Every playthrough of Beyond the Algorithm puts a team through the same kickoff, the same event cards, the same disclosure deadline. What changes is how each team interprets the pressure, negotiates who's responsible for what, and decides what's worth writing down versus leaving out. That's exactly the kind of practical, in-the-moment negotiation that's easy to talk about in an interview afterwards, but hard to actually catch happening — a game lets you watch it happen, and compare it across groups.

It's also a more honest way to get people talking. Roles, turns, and a shared task tend to produce more natural back-and-forth than a formal interview does, and less of the "performing the right answer" that can creep into conversations about ethics and compliance.

In short: the game isn't a teaching tool dressed up as research, and it isn't a research survey dressed up as a game. It's a way of asking the same real question — how do people practise transparency under pressure? — and getting an answer you can only get by watching people actually try.

The game is a research instrument as much as it is a game. Sessions are run with participants' informed consent, and what is recorded is explained in advance. Findings are reported as patterns across teams, never as an assessment of an individual organisation.

Developing it

The game did not arrive finished. It went through several rounds of design, playtesting and rebuilding, each one changing what the teams argue about and how hard the trade-offs bite.

Every version was played through before it was taken anywhere near a real inspectorate.

Where it has been played

Sessions have been run with teams inside Dutch inspection authorities, and at conferences where that work gets discussed.
